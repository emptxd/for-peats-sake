💧 What Is Wet Farming?

Paludiculture is a form of wetland agriculture that makes use of water-saturated soils to grow and harvest plant biomass — without draining the land. Unlike conventional farming, which dries out peat soils and causes CO₂ emissions, paludiculture embraces water to turn wetlands into productive, climate-friendly landscapes.

1. Spontaneous Wetland Cultures

These arise naturally after rewetting the land and adapt over time to local conditions. They include:

  • Wet meadows (formed through summer mowing or light grazing)
  • Wet pastures (used for water buffalo, cattle, etc.)
  • Reed stands (formed via winter mowing and managed growth)

These are diverse, low-maintenance systems that reflect local ecology.

2. Planted Wetland Crops

More structured and active—farmers plant specific species in monoculture or designed mixes. Examples:

  • Common reed (Phragmites australis)
  • Cattails (Typha spp.)
  • Alder trees
  • Wetland grasses

These systems require more setup, permits, and ongoing management, but can yield higher, more targeted biomass outputs.

Types of Cultivations

Reed & Grasses (e.g. Phragmites, Typha, Phalaris, Carex)

  • Site prep: Close drainage pipes, build bunds/dikes/roads, terrace slopes, install irrigation infrastructure.
  • Water mgmt: Controlled inlets, irrigation ditches, regulated overflows; optional pumping (grid or solar).
  • Establishment:
    • Phalaris seed: 15–25 kg/ha, rows 12.5 cm apart.
    • Plant reed: 0.25–4 plants/m²; cattail: <2 seedlings/m².
    • Use pelletized seed or hand/mechanical planting.
  • Maintenance: Mow bunds 1+×/yr; perform establishment cuts; check irrigation gear.
  • Harvest: Annual after 1–3 yr; tracked harvesters, bales or chaff; future autonomous kit.
  • Yield:
    • Reed: 6–24 t DM/ha·yr (3–15 t winter).
    • Cattail: 4–22 t DM/ha·yr.
    • Phalaris: 1.6–13 t DM/ha·yr.

Peat Moss (Sphagnum spp.)

  • Site prep: Remove nutrient-rich topsoil, level peat surface, build bunds/roads for access.
  • Water mgmt: Create hydrologic units with cascades, install pumps & sensors, dig irrigation ditches, use adjustable overflows.
  • Establishment: Transplant live moss to ~80% cover (~80 m³/ha) early in the growing season.
  • Maintenance: Mow to suppress vascular plants, clean ditches, service pumps, mulch bunds.
  • Harvest: Every 3–5 yr with tracked excavators; future autonomous light machinery.
  • Yield: ~2–8 t DM/ha·yr.

Black Alder (Alnus glutinosa)

  • Site prep: Ensure free upstream flow, build ridges to protect seedlings, mow existing vegetation.
  • Water mgmt: Maintain free inflow; avoid prolonged inundation.
  • Establishment: Plant 2-yr nursery seedlings at 3 000–3 500/ha (2 × 2 m spacing), on ridges for wetlands.
  • Maintenance: Juvenile care with first thinning at ~8 yr, 4–6 thinnings before final harvest.
  • Harvest: Low-impact methods (e.g. cable winch) for thinnings; full rotation at 60–80 yr.
  • Yield: 600–800 m³ standing volume per ha at age 60.

Understanding the Metrics

  • 53 Mt CO₂e/yr: Drained peatlands in Germany emit 53 million tonnes of CO₂-equivalent annually—about 6% of the country’s total greenhouse-gas emissions.
  • 0.23 Nm³/kg DM: One kilogram of dried wetland biomass can yield ~0.23 normal cubic meters of methane in a biogas digester—enough to run a small stove for 10–15 minutes.
  • 6–20 t DM/ha: Paludiculture systems produce between 6 and 20 tonnes of dried plant material per hectare annually—equivalent to several dump trucks of biomass.

What is Global Warming Potential?

Global warming potential (GWP) measures the net greenhouse-gas flux of a land use in tons of CO₂-equivalent per hectare per year. Positive values mean the land emits more greenhouse gases than it absorbs; negative values would indicate a net sink. This table shows how different paludiculture systems compare:

  • 0–7 t CO₂-eq/ha·yr for common reed means it can be nearly carbon neutral to slightly emitting.
  • 6–7 t CO₂-eq/ha·yr for cattail shows moderate emissions when harvested regularly.
  • Systems like wet pasture with buffalo (8–12 t CO₂-eq/ha·yr) still outperform conventional grassland (31.7) or cropland (40.4).

Key Benefits of Paludiculture

🔥

Climate Change Mitigation

Rewetting peatlands for paludiculture significantly reduces CO₂ emissions, potentially turning peat soils back into carbon sinks.

🌱

Sustainable Biomass Utilization

Wetland biomass yields 0.21–0.26 Nm³ CH₄/kg DM for biogas and provides peat-free growing media using Typha and other plants.

🐦

Biodiversity & Ecosystem Services

Restored peatlands support rare wetland species and improve water quality via nutrient retention (N&P), enhancing overall habitat health.

💼

Economic Potential

Biomass yields of 6–20 t DM/ha generate revenue streams for bioenergy and raw materials, reducing import dependency and boosting rural incomes.

💧

Nutrient Management & Soil Quality

Paludiculture buffer zones trap nitrogen and phosphorus, reducing eutrophication, while rewetting restores soil structure and health.

🤝

Regional Development & Employment

New industries around biomass harvesting and processing create jobs in rural regions, driving economic diversification and growth.

⛈️

Adaptation to Climate Impacts

Rewetted peatlands buffer floods and droughts by stabilizing water levels, increasing resilience to extreme weather events.
